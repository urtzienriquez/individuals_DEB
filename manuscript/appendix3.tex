\section{Jensen bias for males}

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:map-jensen-M}} shows the spatial distribution of Jensen bias ($J$) between population-average and individual-level prediction surfaces for males under current European climate. While the population-average DEB parameterisation underestimated most performance metrics for females (\textbf{Fig.~2}), the pattern for males was largely opposite: the population-average parameterisation overestimated most metrics, including reproductive energy ($|J| = 2315$\,J), basking time ($|J| = 286$\,h) and foraging time ($|J| = 398$\,h). In relative terms, male bias was largest for reproductive energy (normalised $|J| = 9.1$\%), $R_0$ (7.4\%) and food intake (6.9\%). Overestimation dominated for body size, reserve, food, reproductive energy and basking metrics ($J>0$ in 57--96\% of cells), whereas maximum and minimum body temperature showed predominantly negative bias ($J<0$ in 84--93\% of cells). The magnitude and direction of Jensen bias therefore depended strongly on both sex and ecological performance metric.

\section{Coefficient of variation maps}

The coefficient of variation (CV) across the 37 individual-level predictions quantifies the degree of physiological heterogeneity independent of Jensen bias. High CV values identify regions and metrics where individual variation most strongly influences predicted outcomes.

\subsection{Females}

For females, CV was highest toward the range margins for most performance metrics (\textbf{Fig.~\ref{fig:map-cv}}), indicating that the ecological consequences of individual physiological variation were spatially structured rather than uniform across the landscape.

\section{Climate warming reshapes Jensen bias (males)}

Climate warming altered the magnitude of Jensen bias for all twelve male performance metrics (\textbf{Fig.~\ref{fig:map-jensen-warming-M}}), mirroring the pattern observed for females (\textbf{Fig.~3}). However, as for females, warming did not produce a uniform response: the strongest relative amplification occurred for minimum body temperature (rel.\ $\Delta|J| = +$510\%; 60\% of cells) and maximum body temperature (rel.\ $\Delta|J| = +$215\%; 62\% of cells), whereas lifespan ($-1\%$) was attenuated. The sign of $J$ changed in 5--46\% of cells under \qty{+4}{\celsius} warming, with highest rates for basking time (46\%), foraging time (43\%) and minimum reserve (31\%). Even under \qty{+2}{\celsius} warming, the direction of change was already discernible for most metrics: 11 metrics showed increased $|J|$, with relative changes ranging from $+$10\% (maximum body length) to $+$126\% (minimum body temperature), while 1 metric was attenuated (minimum reserve ($-1\%$)), and 1 metric (lifespan) switched from amplification at \qty{+2}{\celsius} to attenuation at \qty{+4}{\celsius}, suggesting a nonlinear threshold in the averaging bias. For comparison, in females 1 metric was attenuated under \qty{+2}{\celsius} (minimum reserve ($-5\%$)).

\section{Consequences for ecological forecasts (males)}

Jensen bias produced trait-specific differences between population-average and individual-level forecasts for males under both current and future climates (\textbf{Fig.~\ref{fig:map-suitability-M}}), mirroring the female pattern (\textbf{Fig.~4}). Reproductive energy exhibited the largest discrepancies, with the population-average model generally overpredicting relative to the individual-level mean under the current climate. The population-average model also overpredicted lifespan (84\% of cells; median relative bias $+2.1$\%) and maximum body mass (95\% of cells; median $+5.5$\%), and the median relative bias in reproductive energy under \qty{+4}{\celsius} was $+10.9$\%. Lifespan showed a consistent tendency for the population-average model to predict higher values than the individual-level mean across climate scenarios, whereas maximum body mass showed comparatively close agreement, indicating relatively weak consequences of physiological averaging for this trait. The relative bias maps (\textbf{Figs.~\ref{fig:pct-diff-F}, \ref{fig:pct-diff-M}}) express the same comparison as a percentage of the individual-level mean, making the spatial structure of the disagreement visible.
